Lissajous Curves

Take two oscillations at right angles to one another — one driving horizontal motion, one vertical — and trace the point where they meet. The shape that appears depends almost entirely on the ratio between their frequencies.

What you're looking at

x(t) = sin(a·t + δ)   y(t) = sin(b·t)

Both coordinates are periodic, so the curve closes the moment they return to their starting values together — at t = 2π/gcd(a,b). Only the reduced ratio matters: 4:6 draws exactly the same figure as 2:3, just twice as quickly. Along the way the curve touches the left and right edges of its box a/gcd times and the top and bottom b/gcd times, which is the quickest way to read a ratio straight off a picture.

The phase δ doesn't change that structure, only how the figure is tilted and folded. At δ = 0 the curve degenerates into a single doubled-back arc; a quarter turn later it opens into its widest form. Sweep the slider and watch a 1:1 curve pass from a diagonal line, through an ellipse, to a circle, and back.

Set an irrational ratio and the curve would never close at all — it would fill the square densely, forever. The sliders here are restricted to whole numbers, so every figure you can draw eventually comes home.
